## Summary

This paper argues that recommender systems like Google Scholar, which use a mechanism called stigmergy (leveraging search algorithms), effectively surface paths but often overemphasize popularity. This reliance on "rich-get-richer" dynamics can lead to intellectual homogeneity, amplify structural biases, and stifle diverse scientific perspectives needed for progress by making high-profile papers disproportionately visible. The authors suggest mitigating this issue through user-specific calibration features in search platforms and the strategic use of word embeddings or LLMs, aiming to increase individual control over recommendations.

## Technical note

This essay argues that scholarly recommender systems (Google Scholar, Web of Science) operate via stigmergy—a rich-get-richer preferential-attachment dynamic—that overweights popularity, producing heavy-tailed visibility distributions that foster intellectual homogeneity and structural inequity (e.g., disadvantaging marginalized scholars, citing the Music Lab experiment and the diversity-innovation paradox). It proposes system-level fixes: user-specific calibration letting researchers manually reweight popularity, recency, and relevance, plus stochastic noise injection into popularity scores to disrupt feedback loops. It further suggests using transformer document embeddings for semantic retrieval beyond keyword match and LLMs as linguistic interfaces (not oracles) for natural-language search-parameter control and interactive orchestration, while preserving link persistence, plurality, and transparency. The central recommendation is increasing user autonomy/control over ranking to temper (not flatten) inequality and enlarge scientific exploration.
