## Summary

This paper argues that existing methods for evaluating AI chatbot safety for children are inadequate because they often miss real-world harms caused by specific chatbot responses in high-stakes situations. The study involved interviews with 19 child welfare professionals who identified problematic chatbot behaviors and offered recommendations based on their practical experience with vulnerable youth, going beyond the common "I can't help" refusal standard. These findings suggest that incorporating practitioner perspectives is crucial for developing more effective AI safety evaluations and improving chatbot infrastructure to genuinely support children's well-being.

## Technical note

Microsoft interviews 19 practitioners who work directly with vulnerable youth (licensed social workers, therapists, counselors, psychologists; 50-60 minute sessions, $75 compensation, IRB-approved) to test whether current child-safety benchmarks measure what actually harms children. Probes were built by grounding personas in two real harm corpora -- the Lives Cut Short child abuse/neglect dataset and the Lost Screen Memorial -- generating 100 scenarios with an LLM research agent, selecting 12 spanning mental health, abuse, bullying, eating disorders, relationships and isolation, and pairing each with two qualitatively different real LMArena chatbot responses; the scope deliberately excludes adversarial youth prompts and blatantly harmful outputs. Practitioners flagged failures invisible to content-based benchmarks: chatbots missing context entirely (sycophantically helping a 14-year-old buy a gift for a 33-year-old partner), hand-delivering dangerous specifics (bridge locations after a user mentions failing a test, overdose dosages), advising youth to talk to a parent who may be the abuser, over-long responses younger children will not read, and resource framing ('national sexual assault', 'staff are mandated reporters') that deters help-seeking. Most consequentially, refusal -- the dominant safety proxy in benchmarks -- was consistently judged harmful, a 'missed opportunity' that can reinforce a young person's belief that no help is available. Endorsed behaviors were: gathering context with follow-up questions before advising, naming legal and practical risks, empathetic tone that leaves the door open, and routing to tailored human support with the chatbot acting as a bridge rather than a substitute; the authors recommend evaluating whether a response leaves a user better or worse off (not whether it contains prohibited content), dropping refusal rate as a safety metric, and embedding practitioners throughout evaluation design. Limitations: US-only practitioners, synthetic single-turn probes, and contested clinical judgment (participants disagreed on the same scenarios).
