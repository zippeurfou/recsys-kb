## Summary

Netflix conducted an experiment with millions of users to assess how improving its recommendation algorithms affects content consumption. They found that better recommendations increased total consumption and user reliance on suggestions, while simultaneously diversifying what users watched. Specifically, consumption shifted away from the most popular ("superstar") titles and towards a larger number of moderately popular ("middle-tail") titles, with little effect on the least popular ("long-tail") titles. This result challenges the idea that recommendation systems polarize audiences by boosting only the top hits, suggesting instead that better algorithms can help a wider range of content gain visibility.

## Technical note

Studies how continuous improvements to Netflix's recommender reshape which titles get consumed, using a 60-day cumulative-holdback experiment on 8,559,252 subscribers (Feb-Apr 2025) where control keeps a frozen algorithm and treatment receives twelve algorithmic releases. Titles are bucketed by aggregate play percentile into superstar (top 5%), middle-tail, and long-tail (bottom 50%); within-user recommendation and play shares are regressed on treatment. Improvements shift both recommendations and plays away from superstars toward the middle-tail with near-zero long-tail change: recommendation HHI falls 5.7% and play HHI 1.2%. Engagement rises (view hours +0.37%, plays +0.62%, distinct titles +1.2%, days-with-play +0.21%), plays-per-visit +0.47%, share of first-session plays from recommendations +0.5%, MRR +0.1%, and users complete 1.3% more titles; average per-play match quality is roughly flat to slightly negative, which the authors attribute to extensive-margin entry (Lee bounds cannot sign it). A stylized model with a popularity default explains the hump-shaped redistribution as better inference from limited data for middle-tail titles.
