## Summary

This paper introduces **Structure-Aligned History Learning (SAHL)** to improve generative recommendation by addressing two main flaws in existing methods: imbalanced semantic ID assignments and insufficient supervision for reconstructing detailed user history. SAHL uses a novel **Semantic Residual Balanced Quantization (SRBQ)** technique to create a token-subspace-item hierarchy directly from the quantization process, enabling structured history reconstruction. The model then leverages predictive entropy and curriculum optimization to focus on uncertain transitions and gradually align training towards accurate next-item prediction, resulting in significant performance gains (NDCG@10 improvement of 18.6%-30.0%) on Amazon Review datasets compared to prior approaches.

## Technical note

Apple's SAHL targets two gaps in semantic-ID generative recommendation: quantizers like OPQ yield imbalanced codeword occupancy and no coherent unit between a single token and a whole item, and next-item training gives no explicit supervision for recovering persistent preferences spread across distant history. Semantic Residual Balanced Quantization (SRBQ) whitens and PCA-decorrelates Sentence-T5 item embeddings, splits dimensions into G subspaces, applies D-level residual K-means per subspace and caps cluster occupancy, producing a token-subspace-item hierarchy; SAHL then masks history at exactly those token, subspace and item granularities and adds a masked-reconstruction loss to the parallel next-item loss (RPG-style GPT-2 backbone), selecting targets by predictive entropy of the following transition and using a curriculum (random-masking warm-up, entropy-guided masking with decaying ratio, then pure next-item prediction to match inference). On four Amazon Reviews 2014 categories SAHL improves NDCG@10 by 18.6-30.0% over RPG, 3.4-24.1% over Tlow and 2.7-6.0% over MHL (Beauty N@10 0.0517 vs 0.0495), SRBQ cuts mean/worst peak codeword load by 28.8-32.4%/37.3-41.3%, and controls show that reusing actual SRBQ groups beats same-size artificial groups and that shuffling histories hurts.
