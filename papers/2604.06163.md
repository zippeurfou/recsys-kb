## Summary

Neural retrievers often show a source bias, favoring texts generated by large language models (LLMs) over semantically similar human-written texts. This bias is not an inherent model flaw but stems from imbalances in the training data used for retrieval, specifically artifacts like fluency and term specificity present in both the positive (LLM) and negative (human) documents. The paper shows that contrastive learning during training inevitably absorbs these data biases, leading to the observed preference. To counteract this, the authors propose two methods: modifying the training data to reduce artifact differences or adjusting LLM text vectors to remove their bias projection.

## Technical note

This paper argues neural retrievers' source bias (preferring LLM-generated over semantically-equivalent human text) is not architectural but induced by relevance supervision: relevance-supervised retrievers show consistent bias while general-purpose and unsupervised (Contriever, SimCSE) models do not, and fine-tuning unsupervised models on MS MARCO induces the bias. Analysis of 14 datasets shows positives-vs-negatives differ in non-semantic artifacts (lower perplexity, higher IDF), mirroring LLM-vs-human differences; in embedding space the positive-negative direction aligns (>3-sigma) with the LLM-human displacement. A theoretical decomposition (Propositions 1-2) shows contrastive InfoNCE training necessarily absorbs artifact imbalance as an approximately linear component of the score. Two mitigations validate the mechanism: in-batch-only negative sampling (reducing training artifact imbalance) and inference-time projection removing the LLM-human direction, both cutting bias with minimal NDCG loss.
