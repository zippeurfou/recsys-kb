## Summary

The paper introduces "Survival Game," a novel evaluation framework that measures intelligence by counting trial-and-error failures in solving tasks. Fewer failures indicate higher intelligence, and this method defines an "Autonomous Level" signifying consistent success across diverse challenges like vision or language.

Current AI systems achieve the Autonomous Level on simple problems but struggle significantly with complex ones due to their reliance on mimicry rather than deep task understanding.

Reaching the required performance level necessitates models that grasp underlying mechanisms, which would demand an astronomical number of parameters (around 10^26) and far exceed any feasible computational resources or time investment under Moore's Law.

## Technical note

The paper proposes Survival Game, a framework that evaluates intelligence objectively by the number of failed attempts in a trial-and-error process, modeling failure count as a discrete random variable and defining an Autonomous Level when both expectation and variance are finite. Evaluating state-of-the-art AI, it finds systems reach the Autonomous Level on simple tasks but remain at a Limited Level (comparable to brute-force enumeration) on complex tasks including vision, search, recommendation, and language. An observed log-linear score-vs-scale relationship projects that autonomous general language ability would require ~10^26 parameters, an astronomically infeasible cost. A theoretical analysis via self-organized criticality argues human tasks have a criticality property, so superficial imitation without mechanistic understanding cannot reach autonomy affordably.
