## Summary

This paper investigates how recommender systems' system exposure data (the items presented to users) can inadvertently reveal users' private past behavior. The authors developed an attack model showing that observing current recommendations allows inferring users' historical clicks, posing a significant privacy risk due to the wide accessibility of exposure data. To mitigate this, they propose a two-stage mechanism that modifies the recommended slate to reduce leakage, though this comes with a trade-off between privacy protection and recommendation accuracy.

## Technical note

This paper identifies a novel privacy risk: a user's private historical clicks can be inferred from the system's exposure (recommendation slate) data, which is higher-volume and more widely accessible than protected user-behavior logs. The authors build a black-box encoder-decoder attack that takes current exposed items as input and reconstructs past user behavior, testing mean/max pooling and self-attention encoders with point-wise (multi-label) and sequence-wise (GRU/LSTM/transformer) decoders. Experiments on two real-world datasets show a high risk of leakage. They propose a two-stage protection that selects a subset of exposed items and replaces them with uniform or popularity-based exposure, revealing a trade-off between recommendation accuracy and privacy disclosure.
