## Summary

This paper addresses challenges in fine-tuning generative recommenders using sparse and noisy logged feedback. The authors propose Exponential reward-weighting (Exp-RSFT), which optimizes directly on these logged interactions by assigning weights based on $\exp(r/λ)$. They theoretically demonstrate that performance depends crucially on the temperature parameter $λ$, balancing trade-offs between exploiting high-reward data and mitigating noise, showing an inverted-U relationship. Empirically, Exp-RSFT consistently outperforms standard methods like PPO/DPO across benchmarks by avoiding unreliable reward over-optimization without needing online exploration or preference data.

## Technical note

Post-training a generative recommender with RLHF-style methods fails in the recsys regime because a reward model fit on sparse logged feedback over a huge catalog does not generalize, so PPO/DPO reward-hack it; propensities for IPS are unavailable under complex production logging policies and there is no online exploration. Exp-RSFT instead does plain SFT with each logged trajectory weighted by exp(R(tau)/lambda) - the closed-form solution of KL-constrained reward maximization, i.e. an exponential tilt of the behavior policy - requiring no reward model, no propensities, and no value function. The theory decomposes suboptimality into a coverage cost that grows only logarithmically in the inverse probability of the optimal trajectory under the logging policy and a noise cost that grows only logarithmically in catalog size; the two move in opposite directions in lambda, giving a closed-form lambda* and predicting an inverted-U in performance. Empirically, with HSTU as the base recommender on ML-1M, ML-20M, Amazon Books, and a proprietary StreamCo dataset, the inverted-U appears with an optimum around lambda ~ 0.5-1.0, and Exp-RSFT beats BC, linear RSFT, PPO, and DPO (e.g. ML-20M NDCG@10 0.1912 vs 0.1753 BC / 0.1847 RSFT; StreamCo +106.2% NDCG@10 over RSFT while DPO is -99.3%). Reward hacking is demonstrated directly: the learned reward model does not beat an Item-Mean predictor on MSE/MAE, and on every dataset the method with the highest reward-model-judged Avg Reward is the one with the worst ranking metrics.
